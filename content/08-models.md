## One line

These measures compare series through a fitted model, a complexity estimate, the distribution of order patterns or a compressor, and the real decision is the step that comes before the distance: fit, embed or encode.

## What it measures

A model-based distance fits a parametric model to each series and compares the fitted models, not the data. A complexity-based distance compares how intricate the series are, or how much information they share. Two series generated by the same process can then be close with entirely different values, which is what [Montero and Vilar (2014)](https://doi.org/10.18637/jss.v062.i01) mean by a structure-based dissimilarity: it compares underlying dependence structures and not geometric profiles.

The five measures of this lesson, in order:

- **AR distance of [Piccolo (1990)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9892.1990.tb00048.x).** Euclidean distance between the autoregressive expansions of the fitted models.
- **Cepstral distance of [Kalpakis, Gada and Puttagunta (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICDM.2001.989529).** Euclidean distance between the linear predictive coding cepstrum coefficients derived from the autoregressive fit.
- **CID of [Batista et al. (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10618-013-0312-3).** A raw-data distance multiplied by a correction that grows with the difference in complexity of the two series.
- **Ordinal patterns.** [Bandt and Pompe (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.88.174102) describe a series by the relative frequency of the order patterns of short windows. Brandmaier (2011) turns this into a distance between series.
- **Normalized compression distance of [Cilibrasi and Vitányi (2005)](https://doi.org/10.1109/TIT.2005.844059).** How much less space two series need together than apart.

![Relative frequency of the six ordinal patterns of length 3 in mon, a two-peak day, and sun, a flat-block day](figures/ordinal-patterns.svg)

On the fixture, an authored demonstration, the normalised permutation entropy of `mon` is {{n:pe_mon}} and that of `sun` is {{n:pe_sun}}, both close to the maximum of 1. At this pattern length a two-peak day and a flat-block day look almost alike, so the length is a choice and not a detail.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- The question is about dynamics: persistence, volatility behaviour, regularity, predictability. Montero and Vilar note that a structure-based dissimilarity is often the appropriate kind for economic or financial indicators.
- Series differ in length. A model or a distribution of patterns is comparable whatever the length. Caiado and Crato use this for volatilities estimated on samples of different sizes.
- Level and scale should not count. The AR distance, the cepstral distance and the ordinal patterns ignore them. Montero and Vilar describe the permutation distribution as invariant to differences in mean and variance and robust to drift.

Avoid it when:

- The shape of the profile is the question. A fitted model discards the profile, and two series with the same dynamics but different shapes are close.
- The series cannot be made stationary and linear well enough for the model to be a fair description.
- Series are short. The embedding dimension of the ordinal approach needs the number of patterns to be small against the series length.

## Definition

**AR distance.** Fit an autoregressive model of order $k_x$ and $k_y$ to each series, after differencing if it is not stationary, with the order chosen by AIC or BIC. With $\hat\pi_{j, x}$ the coefficients, padded with zeros up to $k = \max(k_x, k_y)$, [Piccolo (1990)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9892.1990.tb00048.x) defines on invertible ARIMA processes the Euclidean distance between their $\mathrm{AR}(\infty)$ operators, in practice the truncated ones:

$$
d_{\mathrm{PIC}}(x, y) = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{k} \big(\hat\pi_{j, x} - \hat\pi_{j, y}\big)^2}
$$

It does not use the variance of the innovations. [Maharaj (2000)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s003570000023) instead takes as a dissimilarity a test statistic, or its p-value, for the null that two stationary processes have the same AR coefficients. That statistic does use the innovation variances, so it is affected by the unit of measurement.

**Cepstral distance.** With the AR coefficients $\phi_1, \dots, \phi_p$, the linear predictive coding cepstrum is, for $h \ge 1$ and $\phi_m = 0$ beyond $p$:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\psi_h = \phi_h + \sum_{m=1}^{h-1} \Big(1 - \frac{m}{h}\Big) \phi_m \psi_{h-m} \\[6pt]
d_{\mathrm{LPC}}(x, y) = \sqrt{\sum_{h=1}^{H} \big(\psi_{h, x} - \psi_{h, y}\big)^2}
\end{gathered}
$$

Kalpakis et al. argue that a few of these coefficients keep much of the information on the underlying ARIMA model.

**CID.** Batista et al. multiply a raw distance $d$, in practice the Euclidean one, by a complexity correction, with $CE$ a complexity estimate:

$$
\begin{gathered}
d_{\mathrm{CID}}(x, y) = d(x, y) \cdot \frac{\max\{CE(x), CE(y)\}}{\min\{CE(x), CE(y)\}} \\[6pt]
CE(x) = \sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T-1} (x_t - x_{t+1})^2}
\end{gathered}
$$

When both series have the same complexity, the factor is 1 and CID equals $d$.

**Permutation entropy and ordinal distance.** Take windows of $d$ consecutive values and record, for each, the permutation that sorts it. With $p(\pi)$ the relative frequency of the pattern $\pi$ among the $n - d + 1$ windows, the normalised permutation entropy of [Bandt and Pompe (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.88.174102) is:

$$
H(x) = -\frac{1}{\ln d!} \sum_{\pi} p_x(\pi) \ln p_x(\pi)
$$

It is 1 when all $d!$ patterns are equally frequent. Permutation distribution clustering compares two series by a divergence between their pattern distributions, $D(p_x, p_y)$. Brandmaier uses an $\alpha$-divergence, which generalises the Kullback-Leibler divergence and can be made symmetric.

**NCD.** With $C(\cdot)$ the compressed size under a given compressor and $xy$ the concatenation of the two encoded series:

$$
d_{\mathrm{NCD}}(x, y) = \frac{C(xy) - \min\{C(x), C(y)\}}{\max\{C(x), C(y)\}}
$$

::: deep What NCD approximates
The normalized information distance of Li et al. (2004) uses the Kolmogorov complexity $K$, the length of the shortest program that produces an object, and is a metric with values in $[0, 1]$. $K$ cannot be computed. NCD replaces it by the output of a real compressor and uses $K(x \mid y) \approx K(xy) - K(y)$. Cilibrasi and Vitányi show that NCD satisfies the metric properties and approximates optimality for a compressor that is "normal" in their sense, and argue that all standard compressors are asymptotically normal. Real compressors have flaws, so the values can reach slightly above 1.

Keogh, Lonardi and Ratanamahatana (2004) propose a simpler compression-based dissimilarity, which has a range from 1/2 for identical objects to 1, and which is not a metric.
:::

## Fit, embed or encode

Each family needs a step before the distance, and that step is the decision.

- **Fit.** The model-based distances assume that the series come from stationary linear processes. If a series is not stationary Piccolo differences it, and a seasonal pattern is removed first. The order of the model comes from an information criterion. Montero and Vilar warn that measures with input parameters can produce an undesirable variability of results. For volatility, [Caiado and Crato (2007)](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/2074/) measure distances between the parameters of GARCH models fitted to the return series.
- **Embed.** For ordinal patterns the embedding dimension $d$ is the single parameter. A small $d$ gives a distribution with little representational power, and a large $d$ gives unreliable frequencies, because $d! \ll n$ must hold for a reliable estimate.
- **Encode.** A compressor works on a string. A real-valued series must be written as one, and Brandouy et al. (2015) show that the way a return series is encoded can create spurious compression.

## Which clustering algorithm

All of these give a pairwise dissimilarity or a vector of coefficients, and none gives a natural centroid of a set of series:

- **Agglomerative hierarchical.** Needs only the matrix. With Maharaj's p-values, a significance level $\alpha$ acts as a homogeneity criterion: series whose dynamic structures do not differ significantly at that level are grouped. Ward's criterion assumes Euclidean distances between objects.
- **k-medoids.** Works on any dissimilarity matrix, and each centre is a real series (Kaufman and Rousseeuw, 1990).
- **k-means.** Needs a centroid, and Montero and Vilar note that the centroid of a set of series may not be properly defined for these dissimilarities.
- **DBSCAN.** Runs on a precomputed matrix (Ester et al., 1996), with its radius set on the scale of the chosen dissimilarity.
- **k-Shape.** Not a match: it is built on SBD (Paparrizos and Gravano, 2015).

## In finance

These measures answer whether two assets share the same dynamics, the same degree of regularity or the same predictability, not whether they move together on the same dates. For same-date dependence the literature offers several measures: Pearson correlation for linear association, the rank correlations of [Spearman (1904)](https://doi.org/10.2307/1412159) and [Kendall (1938)](https://doi.org/10.1093/biomet/30.1-2.81) for monotonic association, dynamic conditional correlation for time-varying dependence ([Engle, 2002](https://doi.org/10.1198/073500102288618487)), and copulas for dependence beyond correlation ([Embrechts, McNeil and Straumann, 2002](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511615337.008)).

Empirical applications:

- **Volatility clustering of stock markets.** [Caiado and Crato (2007)](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/2074/) apply a GARCH-based distance to daily returns of major international stock markets from 1966 to 2006, with samples of different sizes. Most European markets, the United States and Canada are close together, and most Asian and Pacific markets and the South and Middle American markets form a separate cluster. The paper is a working paper.
- **Inefficiency of stock markets.** [Zunino et al. (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.physa.2009.03.042) propose the number of forbidden patterns and the normalised permutation entropy as indicators of stock market inefficiency. They find that more inefficiency goes with more forbidden patterns and lower permutation entropy.
- **Commodity futures.** [Zunino et al. (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.physa.2010.11.020) rank twenty commodity futures markets from about 1991 to 2009 by efficiency, with permutation entropy and statistical complexity.
- **Sovereign bonds.** [Zunino et al. (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.physa.2012.04.009) rank thirty sovereign bond indices by efficiency in the same way. The classification matches the sovereign ratings of the agencies, and permutation entropy correlates with economic development and market size.
- **Time-varying efficiency.** [Alves et al. (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-78707-2) compute permutation entropy over sliding windows of log-returns of 43 stock indices, with $d = 4$ and a window of 500 trading days. Markets form clusters with similar long-term efficiency profiles.
- **Compression and the Dow Jones.** [Brandouy, Delahaye and Ma (2015)](https://doi.org/10.3233/af-150052) estimate the Kolmogorov complexity of daily Dow Jones returns with lossless compression. After erasing the well-known stylised facts they find a very high complexity, which they read as support for the efficient market hypothesis.
- **Stock market similarity with CID.** [Wu, Shang and Chen (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.physa.2019.04.080) use the complexity-invariant distance as an exponent in a multifractal large deviation spectrum, and apply it to measure similarity and correlation between stock markets.

Limitations in financial time series:

- **Stationary linear processes.** Montero and Vilar (2014) state that the model-based measures of their package apply only to series with stationary and linear underlying processes. The statement is general and not specific to finance.
- **Series length.** Alves et al. (2020), on stock indices, note that $d! \ll n$ must hold for a reliable estimate of the pattern distribution, and use a window of 500 days with $d = 4$.
- **Stability of the groups.** In Alves et al. (2020), the efficiency ranks and the clusters of markets with similar trends are stable only over short periods, typically a few months.
- **Compression tools and encoding.** Brandouy et al. (2015) write that lossless compression tools were developed for text files and are not designed for financial data, so their use for detecting patterns may be restricted. They also explain that writing returns in base 10 as text gives spurious compression even on random strings, and discretise the returns into integers written in binary.
- **Backtest overfitting.** The higher the number of configurations tried, the greater the probability that a backtest is overfit, and analysts rarely report that number ([Bailey et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1090/noti1105)). The model order, the embedding dimension, the window and the compressor are all such configurations.

## Common mistake

Fitting an AR or GARCH model to a price level and comparing coefficients, or compressing series without fixing the encoding first. The distance then measures the non-stationarity or the encoding, not the dynamics. Make the series stationary, choose the order or the embedding dimension from a stated criterion, and report them with the distance.
